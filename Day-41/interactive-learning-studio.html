Module 1 • Foundation

Think Like a Security Professional

Build the mental model behind cybersecurity.

What is cybersecurity?

Cybersecurity is the practice of protecting systems, networks, applications, devices, and information from unauthorized access, disruption, manipulation, or destruction.

Simple analogy: A digital system is like a building. Authentication is the door check, authorization is which rooms you may enter, encryption is a locked safe, and monitoring is the security camera.

The CIA Triad

The three classic security goals are:

  • Confidentiality — only authorized people can see information.
  • Integrity — information stays accurate and unaltered.
  • Availability — authorized users can access systems when needed.

Risk = likelihood × impact

Security teams prioritize risks rather than treating every issue equally. A low-likelihood event with catastrophic impact can still deserve attention, as can a highly likely event with moderate impact.

Example: An exposed administrator password is often more urgent than a cosmetic website bug because compromise could have a much larger impact.

Misconceptions

  • “Cybersecurity is only an IT problem.” — People and processes matter too.
  • “Strong passwords solve everything.” — Attackers can exploit software, sessions, devices, and people.
  • “We are too small to be targeted.” — Automated attacks can target organizations at scale.

Mini Exercise: Identify the Security Goal

A company encrypts a customer database so a stolen hard drive cannot reveal its contents. Which CIA goal is primarily protected?

Key Takeaways

  • Security is about reducing risk, not achieving perfect safety.
  • The CIA triad gives you a simple way to classify security objectives.
  • People, technology, and processes interact to create security outcomes.

Module 2 • Threats

Understand How Attacks Happen

Recognize common attack patterns and the weaknesses they exploit.

Threat, vulnerability, exploit

A threat is something capable of causing harm. A vulnerability is a weakness. An exploit is a technique or code used to take advantage of a vulnerability.

Phishing

Phishing uses deceptive messages to manipulate a target into clicking a link, opening an attachment, sharing information, or performing an action.

Red flags: urgency, unexpected attachments, suspicious domains, unusual payment requests, and requests to bypass normal procedures.

Malware families

  • Ransomware: disrupts access, commonly through encryption and extortion.
  • Spyware: secretly collects information.
  • Trojan: disguises malicious functionality as legitimate software.
  • Worm: can self-propagate across systems or networks.

Social engineering

Instead of defeating technology directly, an attacker may manipulate a person. Authority, fear, curiosity, urgency, and familiarity are common psychological levers.

Defense habit: pause and independently verify unusual requests.

  • Human manipulation is a major part of the threat landscape.

Module 3 • Defense

Build Layers of Protection

Move from isolated controls to defense-in-depth.

Defense in depth

No single control is perfect. Layer controls so that if one fails, others still reduce the chance or impact of compromise.

MFA and least privilege

MFA adds another verification factor. Least privilege gives users and applications only the access they need.

Analogy: A hotel key card should open your room, not every room in the building.

Encryption

Encryption transforms readable information into protected ciphertext using a cryptographic process. It can protect data in transit and at rest, but it does not automatically protect a compromised account after decryption.

Backups

A backup is useful only if it can actually be restored. Good backup strategy considers frequency, isolation, retention, integrity, and regular recovery tests.

Common Misconception

“If we have antivirus, we're protected.” Modern security requires multiple layers, because attacks can involve identities, cloud services, applications, configurations, and human behavior.

Detection is not proof

An alert is a signal that needs investigation. Analysts correlate evidence, establish scope, identify affected assets, and distinguish false positives from real incidents.

Containment

Containment aims to limit damage and stop spread while preserving enough evidence to understand what happened. Examples include isolating a device or disabling a compromised account.

Recovery

Recovery is more than turning systems back on. Validate systems, restore from trusted sources, monitor closely, and confirm business processes are functioning.

Lessons learned

After an incident, improve controls, update procedures, train people, and record what evidence and decisions mattered. A good incident becomes a source of resilience.

Summary Notes

  • Cybersecurity is a continuous risk-management discipline.
  • Attackers exploit technology and human behavior.
  • Layered defenses reduce both likelihood and impact.
  • Fast, structured incident response can limit damage.
  • Every incident should improve future resilience.
